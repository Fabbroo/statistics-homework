Homework 6 - Statistical Independence

Contingency tables, marginal and conditional frequencies, expected counts, chi-square test and interpretation

1. Theory required

Joint and marginal frequencies

The observed count Oij is the number of observations with X in category i and F in category j. Row and column totals are marginal frequencies.

ni· = Σj Oij,   n·j = Σi Oij,   n = Σi,j Oij

Conditional frequencies

A row-conditional frequency describes the distribution of F given X=i. A column-conditional frequency describes X given F=j.

P̂(F=j | X=i)=Oij/ni·
P̂(X=i | F=j)=Oij/n·j

Independence

In probability, X and F are independent if every joint probability factors into its marginals.

P(X=i,F=j)=P(X=i)P(F=j)

Equivalently, when probabilities are positive, conditioning on X should not change the distribution of F.

Expected frequencies

Under the null hypothesis H₀ of independence, the expected count is obtained from the marginal totals.

Eij = (ni· n·j)/n

Pearson chi-square statistic

χ² = Σi,j (Oij−Eij)²/Eij

Large discrepancies produce a large statistic. With r rows and c columns, the degrees of freedom are (r−1)(c−1).

Decision with the p-value

The p-value is the probability, assuming H₀, of obtaining a chi-square statistic at least as large as the observed one. If p<α, reject H₀. Otherwise, do not reject H₀. This is not proof that independence is true.

Conditions: observations should be independent, categories should be mutually exclusive, and expected counts should normally be at least 5. A common rule is that no expected count is below 1 and at least 80% are at least 5. Sparse tables may require combining defensible categories or using an exact/Monte Carlo method.

3. Interpretation and causality

If the p-value is smaller than α, the sample is inconsistent with the independence model and we report evidence of a statistical association. If it is not smaller, we report that the sample does not provide sufficient evidence to reject independence. We must not write that the variables are “proven independent”.

A chi-square association is not automatically causal. A third variable may affect both variables, selection bias may distort the sample, the direction of influence may be reversed, or the pattern may be accidental. A causal conclusion normally requires a justified study design, especially random assignment in an experiment, and control of confounding variables.
